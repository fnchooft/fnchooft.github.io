As tech giants race to dominate artificial intelligence, a new kind of industrial boom is reshaping communities, power grids and labour markets across the United States. From Northern Virginia to rural Washington state, sprawling data centres packed with power-hungry GPUs are rising at remarkable speed. Supporters say they are building the future. Critics warn they are consuming enormous public resources while primarily enriching a small group of powerful companies.

Analysts estimate that data centres already draw hundreds of terawatt-hours of electricity globally each year, with AI expected to push that demand sharply higher by 2030.

## Building AI in America's backyards

The physical footprint of this boom is highly concentrated. Dense clusters have formed in Northern Virginia's "Data Center Alley", the Dallas–Fort Worth area, greater Phoenix, Chicago, and towns like Quincy in Washington state. New projects tend to land in exurban or rural counties where land is cheaper and zoning rules are more flexible.

Environmental-justice advocates argue that pattern is not accidental. Research and civil-rights campaigns, including an initiative by the NAACP, argue that AI data centres and their associated energy infrastructure are disproportionately sited near lower-income communities and communities of colour. Residents face rising electricity demand, increased air pollution from gas plants and diesel backup generators, and heavy withdrawals from local groundwater for cooling.

"Dirty data centres" have become a rallying point for local organisers. NAACP guidance for "frontline communities" warns that unchecked data-centre growth could strain water resources, worsen air quality and raise utility costs unless stronger community-benefit requirements are enforced.

## The energy paradox

Many of the same governments that have struggled to accelerate clean firm power have moved quickly to approve AI campuses and the transmission lines that feed them. Energy researchers warn that poorly managed growth could slow national decarbonisation if new electricity demand is met with fossil generation rather than renewables and low-carbon baseload.

Some in the nuclear industry see opportunity here. Industry groups argue that small modular reactors (SMRs) could be co-located with data centres to provide reliable, low-carbon power while reducing strain on local grids. Critics question whether serving private data-centre demand should take priority over decarbonising the broader grid and lowering costs for households.

## Layoffs in the age of AI investment

The AI build-out is also reshaping the tech workforce. Several major players leading the AI push have announced large layoffs while ramping up capital expenditure.

Meta underwent multiple rounds of cuts since 2022, eliminating tens of thousands of roles as part of what its leadership called a "year of efficiency", even as the company spent heavily on AI and data-centre expansion. In 2026, reports indicated that nearly 8,000 more employees were affected by a new round of layoffs tied to restructuring around AI priorities.

Microsoft cut up to around 9,000 jobs — roughly 4% of its global workforce — while committing tens of billions of dollars to AI-related infrastructure.

Cisco's restructuring in May 2026 became a flashpoint in that debate. The networking giant cut nearly 4,000 jobs at the same time it reported record quarterly revenue of about $15.8 billion, up roughly 12% year-on-year, and raised its full-year guidance. For critics, it was a striking example of how the AI transition can coincide with record profits and expanding capital investment — while thousands of workers are told their jobs no longer fit the new strategy.

## A growing security question

As more critical services integrate AI systems hosted in the cloud, large data centres become de facto critical infrastructure. That centralisation creates new single points of failure. Cyberattacks, physical incidents or cascading technical problems at major facilities could ripple across dependent systems.

Security analysts have suggested stricter classification of AI data centres as critical infrastructure, greater geographic redundancy, and expanded use of local or edge AI systems to reduce dependence on a few large sites.

## Who is AI for?

As the AI race intensifies, a central question is emerging: who is all of this for? Industry leaders argue that breakthroughs in generative AI will transform productivity and unlock new services. Critics counter that much of the tangible impact has so far been concentrated — in stock prices and capital expenditure — while the environmental and social costs are widely shared.

Civil-rights organisations, environmental advocates and some policy researchers are calling for stronger guardrails: tighter siting rules, community-benefit agreements, tying public incentives to measurable contributions to grid decarbonisation. Others argue for more public or open AI models, and for prioritising applications in clean energy, health, housing and infrastructure over purely commercial use cases.

For now, the build-out continues. Whether these systems ultimately serve the broader public — or primarily the small group of companies that own them — may depend less on what is technically possible than on choices made by policymakers and the public in the years ahead.
