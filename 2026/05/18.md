RFC 6241 describes `<lock>` in roughly two paragraphs. It locks the
candidate or running datastore, prevents concurrent edits, and must be released
with `<unlock>` or by closing the session. That sounds complete. It isn't.

## What the RFC says

Section 7.5 gives you the basic contract: a lock is exclusive, applies to the
whole datastore, and is released when the session terminates. The error cases
cover *lock-denied* (someone else holds it) and *in-use* (an uncommitted
candidate exists). Both are straightforward.

What section 7.5 does not tell you: what happens when a device silently drops
the TCP session while you hold a lock. The lock stays held until the device's
session-timeout fires — which on some platforms defaults to **30 minutes**.

## The caveats that cost me a weekend

**1. Session-timeout is vendor-defined.** RFC 6241 has no MUST about how quickly
a device must reclaim a lock after session loss. One vendor I worked with
defaulted to 1800 seconds. Your orchestrator retries, gets *lock-denied* for
half an hour, and you spend the weekend wondering if the device is broken.

**2. Candidate and running locks are independent.** Locking `candidate` does not
prevent someone else from locking — and `<commit>`-ing from — `running` directly
if the device supports `:writable-running`. Two independent write paths, two
independent lock scopes.

> If your device advertises both `:candidate` and `:writable-running`, you need
> to lock both datastores or accept that you don't have exclusive access.

**3. confirmed-commit interacts with locks in non-obvious ways.** A confirmed
commit starts a rollback timer. If your session drops before the confirming
commit arrives, the device rolls back — but it may or may not release the lock
before doing so. Test this explicitly on every platform you target.

## A defensive locking pattern

The `try ... after` block is non-negotiable. An unhandled exception that leaves
locks held is the kind of bug that surfaces during a maintenance window, not in
your test environment.

## Monitoring lock state with ietf-netconf-monitoring

Most devices expose lock holders via `<get>` on the `ietf-netconf-monitoring`
YANG module (RFC 6022). The `/netconf-state/sessions` subtree shows which
datastore each active session has locked.

---

NETCONF is a well-specified protocol. The RFC is genuinely good. But the
distance between the spec and a working multi-vendor implementation is always
longer than it looks — particularly around failure modes the spec intentionally
leaves to the implementor. Test your lock handling under simulated session loss
before you need to rely on it at 02:00.
